# Empirical Sources & Confidence Levels (v5.1 — 30 Mechanisms + Stylometry Catalog)

Every mechanism in `hook-everything` v5.1 (`M1` through `M30`) plus the stylometry catalog (`REF-1`) is mapped below with its empirical confidence rating and replication status.

> **Bibliographic Verification Note**: Citations and publication metadata in this file were compiled from memory and secondary literature summaries. Always verify exact DOIs, volume/issue numbers, and page ranges against primary academic databases before citing in formal publications.

## Evidence Rating Key

- **`STRONG`** denotes mechanisms supported by independent replications or meta-analyses showing a consistent directional effect in the laboratory or field.
- **`MIXED`** denotes a real theoretical construct or useful practitioner framework where independent replications show high context sensitivity, small or fragile effect sizes, or contested transfer to digital marketing and UX.
- **`WEAK`** denotes mechanisms that failed independent replication in modern meta-analyses, included only to warn against misuse.

## Master Sources Table (M1 - M30 + Stylometry Catalog)

| ID | Mechanism / Concept | Primary Academic Source & Replications | Evidence Rating & Operational Notes |
|---|---|---|---|
| **M1** | **Information Gap Theory of Curiosity** | Loewenstein, G. (1994). *The psychology of curiosity: A review and reinterpretation.* Psychological Bulletin, 116(1), 75-98. | **STRONG**. Curiosity requires baseline knowledge to feel the gap; fails if the gap feels uncloseable or the payoff is trivial. |
| **M2** | **Narrative Transportation** | Green, M. C., & Brock, T. C. (2000). *The role of transportation in the persuasiveness of public narratives.* JPSP, 79(5), 701-721. | **STRONG**. Concrete sensory detail and human stakes absorb cognitive bandwidth and lower counter-arguing. |
| **M3** | **Cognitive Fluency & Disfluency** | Alter, A. L., & Oppenheimer, D. M. (2009). *Uniting the tribes of fluency to form a metacognitive nation.* PSPR, 13(3), 219-235. | **MIXED**. Processing ease increases perceived truth and safety, while strategic disfluency can wake up analytical attention; however, disfluency-induced deeper processing replicates inconsistently across tasks. |
| **M4** | **Schema Violation / Asymmetric Specificity** | Practitioner heuristic informed by schema-discrepancy and surprise-attention literature. | **MIXED**. Practitioner heuristic rather than a single named lab law; concrete, unrounded specifics reliably stop skimming when relevant to the payoff. |
| **M5** | **Fogg Behavior Model (B=MAP)** | Fogg, B. J. (2009, 2019). *Tiny Habits* & behaviormodel.org. | **MIXED**. Practical diagnostic taxonomy (Motivation, Ability, Prompt converging simultaneously) rather than a falsifiable experimental law. |
| **M6** | **Endowed Progress Effect** | Nunes, J. C., & Dreze, X. (2006). *The endowed progress effect: How artificial advancement increases effort.* Journal of Consumer Research, 32(4), 504-512. | **MIXED**. Field study showed 34% vs 19% completion when given an initial head start, but transfer to digital UI checklists backfires whenever the head start feels arbitrary or fake. |
| **M7** | **Goal-Gradient Hypothesis** | Hull, C. L. (1932); Kivetz, R., Urminsky, O., & Zheng, Y. (2006). *The goal-gradient hypothesis resurrected.* Journal of Marketing Research, 43(1), 39-58. | **STRONG**. Effort accelerates as distance to a visible milestone shrinks. |
| **M8** | **Default Effect / Choice Architecture** | Johnson, E. J., & Goldstein, D. (2003). *Do defaults save lives?* Science, 302(5649), 1338-1339. | **STRONG**. Smart defaults dramatically increase adoption without restricting freedom. |
| **M9** | **Ovsiankina Task Resumption** | Ovsiankina, M. (1928); Ghibellini & Meier (2025) Meta-Analysis; Masicampo & Baumeister (2011). | **STRONG**. Interrupted tasks a user intends to finish create a strong resumption urge; forming a concrete plan quiets intrusive open-loop tension. |
| **M10** | **Zeigarnik Recall Claim** | Zeigarnik, B. (1927); Ghibellini & Meier (2025) Meta-Analysis. | **WEAK**. Popular claims that unfinished tasks are remembered better do not reliably replicate in modern meta-analyses. Cite `M9` (Ovsiankina) or `M1` (Information Gap) instead. |
| **M11** | **Generation Effect** | Slamecka, N. J., & Graf, P. (1978). *The generation effect: Delineation of a phenomenon.* JEP: Human Learning and Memory, 4(6), 592-604. | **STRONG**. Information actively generated or inferred by the reader/user is remembered and valued far higher than passively read text. |
| **M12** | **IKEA Effect (Co-Creation & Ownership)** | Norton, M. I., Mochon, D., & Ariely, D. (2012). *The IKEA effect: When labor leads to love.* Journal of Consumer Psychology, 22(3), 453-460. | **MIXED**. Users value artifacts they partially built or customized higher than pre-made ones in lab studies, but the effect vanishes immediately if the task is too hard or fails. |
| **M13** | **Peak-End Rule & Duration Neglect** | Fredrickson, B. L., & Kahneman, D. (1993). *Duration neglect in retrospective evaluations of affective episodes.* JPSP, 65(1), 45-55; 2025 Meta-Analysis (174 effect sizes). | **STRONG**. Retrospective evaluation is dominated by the peak moment and the ending moment. |
| **M14** | **Hooked Framework & Variable Rewards** | Eyal, N. (2013). *Hooked: How to Build Habit-Forming Products*; Ferster & Skinner (1957). | **MIXED**. Useful product loop scaffold (Trigger, Action, Variable Reward, Investment) when grounded in real utility and internal triggers, not a standalone lab law. |
| **M15** | **Psychological Reactance Theory** | Brehm, J. W. (1966). *A theory of psychological reactance.* Academic Press; Rosenberg & Siegel (2018) 50-year review. | **STRONG**. Threatening autonomy triggers resistance and boomerang behavior; explicitly affirming freedom to choose ("You are free to skip") disarms reactance. |
| **M16** | **Self-Determination & Identity-Based Motivation** | Ryan, R. M., & Deci, E. L. (2000). *American Psychologist*, 55(1), 68-78; Oyserman, D., Fryberg, S. A., & Yoder, N. (2007). *JPSP*, 93(6), 1011-1027; Oyserman, D. (2009). *Journal of Consumer Psychology*, 19(3), 250-260. | **MIXED**. Intrinsic motivation (Autonomy, Competence, Relatedness) is well-established, while identity-based motivation (*difficulty-as-importance*) is context-sensitive in consumer persuasion. |
| **M17** | **Persuasion Knowledge Model (PKM)** | Friestad, M., & Wright, P. (1994). *The persuasion knowledge model: How people cope with persuasion attempts.* Journal of Consumer Research, 21(1), 1-31. | **STRONG**. Once a user recognizes a tactic as a "persuasion attempt," they shift into defensive skepticism. Hooks must avoid recognizable marketing templates. |
| **M18** | **Prospect Theory / Loss Aversion** | Kahneman, D., & Tversky, A. (1979). *Econometrica*, 47(2), 263-291; Gal, D., & Rucker, D. D. (2018). *The loss of loss aversion: Will it loom larger than its gain?* Journal of Consumer Psychology, 28(3), 497-516. | **MIXED**. Real owned losses weigh heavily, but Gal & Rucker (2018) show general loss aversion is frequently overstated or confounded with status-quo inertia, and fake scarcity backfires via reactance. |
| **M19** | **ABI Model of Trust (Ability, Benevolence, Integrity)** | Mayer, R. C., Davis, J. H., & Schoorman, F. D. (1995). *An integrative model of organizational trust.* Academy of Management Review, 20(3), 709-734; Colquitt et al. (2007) meta-analysis. | **STRONG**. Trust is the willingness to accept vulnerability when Ability, Benevolence, and Integrity are simultaneously perceived. |
| **M20** | **Pratfall Effect** | Aronson, E., Willerman, B., & Floyd, J. (1966). *Psychonomic Science*, 4(6), 227-228; Helmreich, R., Aronson, E., & LeFan, J. (1970). *To err is humanizing — sometimes: Effects of self-esteem, competence, and a pratfall on interpersonal attraction.* JPSP, 16(2), 262-269. | **MIXED**. Admitting a small flaw can humanize a high-competence actor, but Helmreich et al. (1970) showed the effect depends on observer traits and reverses if competence is not firmly established first. |
| **M21** | **Inoculation Theory (Two-Sided Refutation)** | McGuire, W. J. (1961). *JASP*, 63(2), 326-332; Banas & Rains (2010) meta-analysis. | **STRONG**. Raising a weakened version of the audience's counter-argument and refuting it with data builds lasting immunity against skepticism. |
| **M22** | **Operational Transparency & Costly Signaling** | Buell, R. W., & Norton, M. I. (2011). *The labor illusion: How operational transparency increases perceived value.* Management Science, 57(9), 1564-1579; Buell, Porter & Norton (2020); Spence, M. (1973). *QJE*, 87(3), 355-374; Zahavi, A. (1975); Mishra et al. (1998). | **MIXED**. Costly signaling is theoretically sound, while operational transparency ("labor illusion") increases valuation in specific waiting contexts but fails if waits feel artificial or annoying. |
| **M23** | **Bisociation (Cross-Matrix Creativity)** | Koestler, A. (1964). *The Act of Creation.* Hutchinson & Co; Dubitzky et al. (2012). | **MIXED**. Conceptual creativity model (colliding two orthogonal frames of reference) rather than a controlled experimental effect. |
| **M24** | **Self-Perception Theory & Foot-in-the-Door** | Bem, D. J. (1967). *Psychological Review*, 74(3), 183-200; Freedman, J. L., & Fraser, S. C. (1966). *JPSP*, 4(2), 195-202; Burger, J. M. (1999). *The foot-in-the-door compliance procedure: A multiple-process analysis and review.* PSPR, 3(4), 303-325. | **MIXED**. Voluntary micro-commitments can aid conversion, but Burger's (1999) meta-analysis shows FITD effects are small and fail when the initial step feels coercive or externally rewarded. |
| **M25** | **Mental Accounting & Zero-Price Effect** | Thaler, R. (1985). *Marketing Science*, 4(3), 199-214; Shampanier, K., Mazar, N., & Ariely, D. (2007). *Zero as a special price: The true value of free products.* Marketing Science, 26(6), 742-757. | **MIXED**. Framing costs against existing waste and offering $0 diagnostics reduces friction, though zero-price attraction can draw low-intent users if not paired with qualification boundaries. |
| **M26** | **Regret Regulation & Decision Justification Theory** | Zeelenberg, M. (1999); Zeelenberg, M., & Pieters, R. (2007). *A theory of regret regulation 1.0.* Journal of Consumer Psychology, 17(1), 3-18. | **MIXED**. Anticipated process regret is well-documented in decision research, though direct conversion lift from regret-reversal copy varies by product price and risk level. |
| **M27** | **Von Restorff Isolation Effect** | Von Restorff, H. (1933). *Über die Wirkung von Bereichsbildungen im Spurenfeld.* Psychologische Forschung, 18(1), 299-342. | **STRONG**. A single visually distinctive element in a calm, uniform field captures attention and memory; over-bolding destroys isolation. |
| **M28** | **Choice Overload & Hick's Law** | Iyengar, S. S., & Lepper, M. R. (2000). *When choice is demotivating: Can one desire too much of a good thing?* JPSP, 79(6), 995-1006; Hick, W. E. (1952); Scheibehenne, B., Greifeneder, R., & Todd, P. M. (2010). *Can there ever be too many options? A meta-analytic review of choice overload.* Journal of Consumer Research, 37(3), 409-425. | **MIXED**. Iyengar & Lepper (2000) found 6 jam options converted 30% vs 24 options converting 3%, whereas Scheibehenne et al. (2010) found a near-zero mean effect across 50 studies unless options are uncategorized and users lack prior preferences. |
| **M29** | **Doherty Threshold (<400ms) vs. Labor Perception (>1.5s)** | Doherty, W. J., & Thadani, A. J. (1982). *The economic value of rapid response time.* IBM Systems Journal; Buell, R. W., & Norton, M. I. (2011). *Management Science*. | **MIXED**. Direct manipulation benefits from `<400ms` latency, while showing 1.5s-3.5s of step-by-step work helps only on complex audits where instant results look fake. |
| **M30** | **Elaboration Likelihood (ELM) & Construal Routing** | Petty, R. E., & Cacioppo, J. T. (1986). *The elaboration likelihood model of persuasion.* Advances in Experimental Social Psychology, 19, 123-205; Trope, Y., & Liberman, N. (2010). *Construal-level theory of psychological distance.* Psychological Review, 117(2), 440-463. | **ELM = STRONG; Construal Level Theory = MIXED** (publication-bias-adjusted estimates for CLT are smaller than the original literature). High-scrutiny audiences process via the Central Route (demanding concrete, low-construal proof); low-bandwidth skimmers require a Peripheral-to-Central bridge. |
